Screw Sort guide: rods and top nuts

Learn when to group matching top nuts, when to preserve an empty rod and why an apparently tidy move can leave the next color buried.

Try it on the board →
In this guide
  1. Choose the nut underneath first
  2. Treat an empty rod as a flexible destination
  3. Compare two legal blue moves
  4. A sorted rod is not permanently locked
  5. Spot a loop before it grows
  6. Practice with one spare rod in mind

1. Choose the nut underneath first

Pick a buried color you want to expose, then work backward to the top nut blocking it. If gold is under blue, find a legal blue destination before selecting the source. In the illustrated example, the other blue rod has room, so transferring blue exposes gold without spending the empty rod. Check both the top color and remaining capacity; a matching but full rod cannot help.

2. Treat an empty rod as a flexible destination

An empty rod can accept any top color. Once you place a nut on it, the rod accepts only that color until it is emptied again. Before using your last empty rod, inspect the nut immediately beneath your source top. If that next nut has no legal destination, your move may expose a color without giving it anywhere to go. Several scattered gaps do not always replace one fully empty rod.

3. Compare two legal blue moves

Suppose blue is exposed on your source, another blue rod has one free position, and an empty rod is also available. Grouping blue uses the matching position and preserves the empty rod. That is a useful candidate, but not an automatic best move: examine what sits under the destination’s blue group too. If you later need those buried nuts, you will have to move every blue above them one at a time.

4. A sorted rod is not permanently locked

Progress counts rods that are full and all one color. You can move a top nut off a completed rod under the normal rules; the progress indicator then falls. This is not a scoring error. Reopening a color may be useful to create a matching destination elsewhere, but it adds work. Prefer a move that exposes a needed color over one that merely makes a stack look tidier.

5. Spot a loop before it grows

Moving a nut to an empty rod and then returning it restores the same arrangement while increasing the move count. If you recognize the same top colors and gaps again, stop and identify what your next move is meant to expose. Retry lets you compare openings on the same seed. A failed Hint search is not a proof that the arrangement has no solution, and repeated legal moves are not proof of progress.

6. Practice with one spare rod in mind

In Level 1, try to complete one color while keeping track of where the completely empty rod moves. In Level 2, seven colors and five positions per rod give you more buried dependencies. Before each transfer, say which nut will become exposed and whether it has a destination. Finish when occupied rods are full and single-color; extra rods may stay empty. Speed does not improve the structure of your solution.

Return to the board and test one decision on the same layout before changing the seed. Compare what becomes accessible, not just the move count.

Common questions

Can I transfer several matching nuts in one tap?+

Each move transfers one top nut. If the next nut has the same color, select the source again and transfer it separately, provided the destination still has room.

Is a partly filled single-color rod finished?+

It is useful progress, but the goal requires full single-color rods. Keep checking how many matching nuts remain buried elsewhere before giving up your spare rods.

Why can I keep moving without getting closer to a win?+

Reversible transfers can form a loop. Name the buried color you want to expose before moving, then preserve the space needed for that sequence. A legal move by itself does not guarantee progress.
